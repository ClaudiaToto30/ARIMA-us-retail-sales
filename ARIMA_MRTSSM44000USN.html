ARIMA - U.S. Retail Sales (MRTSSM44000USN)

Claudia Toto

11/17/2026

Data: MRTSSM44000USN (FRED / U.S. Census Bureau) — U.S. Retail Sales: Retail Trade, in millions of dollars, monthly and not seasonally adjusted, from January 1992 to July 2026 (415 observations).

The analysis has two parts, following the class example:

  1. Non-seasonal ARIMA using annual sales (1992–2025).
  2. Seasonal ARIMA using monthly sales.

salesannual <- aggregate(window(sales_ts, end = c(2025,12)), FUN = sum)

Part 1: Non-seasonal ARIMA (annual sales)

plot(salesannual)

Explanation: Annual retail sales grow from about $1.76 trillion in 1992 to about $7.52 trillion in 2025, more than a fourfold increase. The series has a very clear upward trend, so it is not stationary in the mean. There are two visible breaks: the 2008–2009 drop (financial crisis; 2009 was the only year with a sharp decline, ≈ -8.3%) and a strong acceleration after 2020 (post-COVID stimulus and inflation).

ndiffs(salesannual)
## [1] 1
tsdisplay(salesannual)

Explanation: ndiffs() tells us how many differences are needed to make the series stationary (we expect 1 or 2 because of the trend). In tsdisplay, the ACF decays very slowly (the bars stay high across many lags) and the PACF has a dominant first lag: this is the typical signature of a trending series that needs to be differenced.

salesannualdiff1 <- diff(salesannual, differences=1)
plot(salesannualdiff1)

tsdisplay(salesannualdiff1)

Explanation: The first difference represents annual growth in dollars (how much more was sold than the previous year). The trend largely disappears and the series fluctuates around a positive value; the lowest point is 2009 (negative growth) and the highest values appear in 2021–2022. If the ACF/PACF of the differenced series no longer shows significant bars outside the blue lines, one difference is enough.

Explanation: Forecast of annual sales for the next 5 years (2026–2030). The blue line is the point forecast and the shaded band is the 99.5% prediction interval. The band widens with the horizon: the further out, the greater the uncertainty. The forecast continues the series’ upward trend.

#Residual Analysis
Acf(auto_fit$residuals)

Box.test(residuals(auto_fit), lag=20, type="Ljung")
## 
##  Box-Ljung test
## 
## data:  residuals(auto_fit)
## X-squared = 11.849, df = 20, p-value = 0.9212

Explanation: If the model is good, the residuals should be white noise. In the ACF, no bars (or almost none) should cross the blue lines. In the Ljung-Box test, H0 = the residuals are independent: a p-value > 0.05 means we fail to reject H0 and the model captured the structure of the data. (With only 34 years, lag=20 is a high lag; it is kept to follow the class format.)

plot.ts(residuals(auto_fit))

hist(auto_fit$residuals)

tsdiag(auto_fit)

Explanation:

  • plot.ts: the residuals should fluctuate around 0 with no pattern. The largest spikes match 2009 (unexpected drop) and 2021 (post-COVID jump).
  • hist: ideally bell-shaped and centered at 0 (approximately normal residuals). The unusual years create tails.
  • tsdiag: shows in one plot (1) the standardized residuals, (2) the ACF of the residuals, and (3) the Ljung-Box p-values for each lag; the points should be above the dashed 0.05 line.

Part 2: Seasonal ARIMA (monthly sales)

# Seasonal Data
plot(sales_ts)

Explanation: The monthly series shows two components: an upward trend (from ≈ $127 billion in January 1992 to ≈ $677 billion in July 2026) and a very strong seasonal pattern that repeats every year. December is always the peak (≈ 15% above the average month, due to the holiday season) and January–February are the lowest months (≈ 11–12% below average). The April 2020 drop is also visible (COVID shutdowns: from $419 billion in March to $371 billion), followed by a quick recovery in May.

nsdiffs(sales_ts)
## [1] 1
ndiffs(sales_ts)
## [1] 1
ndiffs((diff(sales_ts,12)))
## [1] 1
tsdisplay(diff(diff(sales_ts,12)))

Explanation:

  • nsdiffs() tells us how many seasonal differences (lag 12, because the data are monthly) are needed; we expect 1 given the strong annual pattern.
  • ndiffs() tells us the regular differences needed for the original series.
  • ndiffs(diff(sales_ts,12)) checks whether, after removing seasonality, a regular difference is still needed to remove the trend.
  • tsdisplay(diff(diff(sales_ts,12))) shows the series after both a seasonal and a regular difference. It should be centered at 0 with no trend. Significant bars in the ACF/PACF at lags 1–3 suggest non-seasonal AR/MA terms, and bars at lags 12, 24, 36 suggest seasonal terms (P, Q). The large spike in 2020 is the COVID shock.

Explanation: The ACF of the residuals should have no significant bars, especially at the seasonal lags (12 and 24); if it does, the model did not capture all the seasonality. In Ljung-Box, a p-value > 0.05 indicates the residuals behave like white noise. If the p-value is < 0.05, it is usually due to the 2008–2009 and 2020 shocks, which no ARIMA model can predict.

plot.ts(residuals(fit3))

plot(sales_ts)
lines(fit3$fitted, col = "red")

Explanation:

  • plot.ts(residuals): the residuals fluctuate around 0. The largest errors appear in April–May 2020 (COVID) and late 2008; variability is also larger in recent years because the level of sales is higher.
  • Red line (fitted values): the black line is actual monthly sales and the red line is the fitted values from fit3. The red line reproduces both the trend and the December peaks and January drops, confirming that the model captures the seasonality. The largest gaps between the two lines are in 2020, when the April drop could not be anticipated.
hist(fit3$residuals)

tsdiag(fit3)

Explanation:

  • hist: the residuals should be centered at 0 with an approximately bell shape. The left tail reflects the April 2020 drop.
  • tsdiag: (1) standardized residuals with no pattern, (2) residual ACF within the bands, (3) Ljung-Box p-values by lag above 0.05. If these hold, the seasonal model is adequate for forecasting monthly retail sales.
